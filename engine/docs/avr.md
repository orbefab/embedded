# ATmega328P core

`crates/avr`, package `sim-avr`, is a cycle-counted model of the ATmega328P. The circuit crate holds it behind the active chip seam, and each `atmega328p` part of a netlist runs one. It has no dependencies.

The sources are two documents. DS40002061B is the datasheet, written DS here. DS40002198B is the instruction set manual, written ISM, and its AVRe column gives the cycle counts. A comment in the crate cites one of them, by section, table, or figure.

## What it models

A `Cpu` is one chip. `parse_hex` or `parse_elf` reads an image and `Cpu::load_image` puts it in flash. `Cpu::run_until` runs to a cycle, `Cpu::take_pin` yields each pin change with the cycle it happened on, and `Cpu::drive_pin` drives a pad from outside. `Cpu` is `Clone`, and a clone shares the flash. Cycle 0 is the first instruction, and power-on start-up is already over at that point.

A pin report carries the pin's level and its drive, the pin's own stage apart from the host: `High` or `Low` for an output, `PullUp` for an input with `PORTxn` set and `PUD` clear, and `Off` for an input with no pull-up (Table 14-1). A report goes out when either changes, so a pull-up that turns on under a pin the host already holds high is still a report. While `RSTDISBL` is unprogrammed, `PC6` is `RESET`: its drive is always `PullUp`, its port bits do not move it, and `PINC6` reads 0 (Table 14-7, §14.3.2). An in-run reset keeps what the host drives on the pads.

- The instruction set the datasheet lists for this die, with the hardware multiplier, `MOVW`, and the enhanced `LPM`. Interrupts are Table 12-6, 26 vectors of two words each. Entry takes four cycles (§7.7.1).
- Ports B, C, and D, with the synchronizer on `PINx` (§14.2.4), the pull-ups, and the alternate functions of §14.3 on one pin mux. A pin id is port × 8 + bit, so `PB0` is 0, `PC0` is 8, and `PD0` is 16.
- Timer0, Timer1, and Timer2 in every waveform mode, the shared prescaler of §17, input capture with the noise canceler, and the `T0`/`T1` clock inputs.
- `INT0`, `INT1`, and the three pin-change groups (§13). While `clkI/O` runs, they read each pin through the `PINx` synchronizer, two cycles after the pad changes (Figure 14-3, and Figure 13-1 for a pin change). A pin holds the last level it was driven to in a cycle. Every level that lasts a cycle reaches the edge detector, however many pins change at once. §13.2.1 does not ensure an interrupt for a pulse shorter than a clock, and here it sets no flag. While reset holds the core, through the start-up time-out, the registers keep their reset values (§11.1), so a pin that moves then sets no flag. §11.1 does not say whether the synchronizer samples during reset. This model lets it, so the pin leaves its last level and release flags only a change that reaches the edge detector after it. A first `IN` from `PINx` reads the levels sampled during the hold. A change on the last cycle of the time-out reaches the edge detector after the first instruction. A first `STS` to `EICRA` or `PCMSK2` arms an edge for it only when the host has set the stored register, since reset clears the register file.
- USART0 in asynchronous mode and as an SPI master (§20, §21). SPI master and slave (§19). The TWI master on SCL and SDA (§22), as [The TWI](#the-twi) says.
- The ADC and the analog comparator (§23, §24), sampling voltages the host sets.
- EEPROM (§8), the watchdog (§11), every reset source with its `MCUSR` flag, brown-out, the sleep modes and their wake sources (§10), `PRR`, `CLKPR`, and the fuse bytes.

`Cpu::step` runs one instruction, or enters the interrupt pending at the boundary the core sits on. A host call between two steps lands at that boundary. A `Cpu::poke` that sets a flag under its enable, sets an enable under a flag already up, or unmasks a low level on `INT0` or `INT1`, leaves that interrupt pending there. With `I` set, the next `step` is the four-cycle entry and runs no instruction, and `Cpu::run_until` enters at the same boundary. §7.7.1 serves an interrupt once the current instruction completes, and the poke came after it did, so the flag is already pending when the next instruction would start. After `SEI` or `RETI` one instruction still runs first (§7.7). A host that single-steps passes through every state a run passes through.

A timer's next compare, TOP, or BOTTOM is a cycle number, and a read of `TCNT` works the count out from the cycle. An instruction that ends before that cycle does not step the timer.

`Cpu::run_until` and `Cpu::run_to_news` retire runs of register-only instructions without per-instruction bus work. An op is free when it reads and writes only registers, `SREG` other than setting `I`, `PC` and the cycle count. Loads, stores, stack ops, calls and returns, the I/O bit ops, `SEI`, `SPM`, `SLEEP`, `WDR` and `BREAK` are not free. The run loop first checks three things. The bus must be quiet: no `TCNT` hold, no store waiting for its stamp, no enable window, no CPU halt and no pin report waiting to be flushed. Every instruction's bus tick leaves the bus quiet, so this check guards a `Cpu::poke` between runs, such as one that sets `EERE` and halts the CPU before the next instruction. No interrupt can be taken: none is pending, or `I` is clear. Under `run_to_news`, no news waits. If all three hold, the core runs free ops back to back, and the bus cycle moves once by their total. The op that reaches the next device event or the limit retires the usual way, through the bus tick, the watchdog and sleep checks, and the interrupt check. A bus op ends the run and steps as usual, unless it is a steady load or an idle store, below. No state the host or the firmware can read changes, for these reasons:

- Device state, interrupt lines, the watchdog, sleep, pin reports and the news change only at a device event, on a bus access, or on a host call between runs. A run stops before the first bus access that could change one, and at the op that reaches the next event.
- A free op that ends before the next event takes the tick's fast path. On a quiet bus that path only moves the cycle, so one move by the sum is the same.
- A free op can clear `I` but not set it. So an interrupt that could not be taken when the run began cannot become takeable inside it.
- `SEI` and `RETI` are not free. An interrupt held by their one-instruction delay is pending with `I` set, so the run does not start, and the next instruction steps alone before the entry.

A run also takes the bus ops that change nothing. A load is steady when its read changes nothing, returns what `peek` returns, and returns the same value until the next device event, a store, or a host call. Each device names its steady registers beside its `peek`, with the reason, and a register it does not name is not steady. SRAM, the registers, `SREG`, `SP` and an I/O byte with no device are steady. `TCNTn`, which counts with the cycle, and a 16-bit `ICRnL` read, which latches `TEMP`, are not. Nor are `UDRn`, whose read pops the receive FIFO, `ADCL` and `ADCH`, whose reads lock and unlock the data pair, `SPDR`, `SPSR` while it holds a flag a read would arm for clearing, and `PINx` while a pad change waits out the synchronizer. That last rule is what keeps a run from reading `PINx` across the synchronizer. A change of a pin that `INT0`, `INT1` or a pin-change group watches also queues an `ExtInt` sample for the same cycle, which is an event and ends the run. A pin in no pin-change group (`PC7`) queues none. A store is idle when it would change no state, pin, log entry, notice or schedule. That is an SRAM, register, `SREG` or `SP` store of the value already there, or a `DDRx` or `PORTx` store the port would skip. `IN`, `LDS`, `SBIC` and `SBIS` of a steady address read it through `peek` at the bus cycle the run began on, and an idle `OUT` or `STS` does nothing. Neither changes device state or raises an interrupt, so the reasons above hold for them too.

A run's first op that is not free is its anchor. Each time the program counter comes back to the anchor with the same registers and `SREG` as on the last visit, the stretch between the visits was a pass of a loop that changed nothing. The run adds the cycles of every later whole pass that ends before the next event or the limit, and runs the last pass op by op. A skipped pass is exact, for these reasons:

- A pass is a function of the program counter, the registers, `SREG`, and what its loads return. The stack pointer cannot move, because no op the run takes pushes or pops. Free ops read only registers, `SREG` and flash, and a steady load returns the same value at every cycle before the next event.
- An idle store changes nothing, so SRAM and device state are the same at the start of every pass. Every later pass takes the same path and ends in the same state.
- The run starts only when no interrupt can be taken, and no pass can change that. A steady load and an idle store raise nothing, and `SREG` repeats, so `I` stays as it was.
- The op that reaches the next event or the limit still retires through the bus tick. The event fires on the same edge as it does stepped, and the next load sees its result.
- Nothing carries from one run to the next. The anchor and the state it is compared with live in one run, so a host call between runs cannot leave a stale pass.

A wait on a flag then costs a few passes per event, such as `firmware/analog_serial.S` polling `ADSC`, `UDRE0` and `TXC0`, or `firmware/button.S` copying `PIND2` to `PORTB`. Two common waits gain nothing. A `delay()` built on `micros()` reads `TCNT0`, and `TCNTn` is not steady, so it steps as before. A wait with a timeout counter, such as `while (!flag && --n)`, never repeats its registers, so no pass is skipped, although its steady loads still run inside the run.

A countdown loop changes its registers on every pass, so the steady check never matches it. It is a backward `BRNE` whose body is `NOP`s and one decrement of a counter: `SBIW`, `DEC`, or `SUBI` and up to three `SBCI` of 0 on the next registers up. `firmware/blink.S` and `firmware/fade.S` wait in a three-byte `SUBI`/`SBCI` loop, and `firmware/analog_serial.S` in an `SBIW` loop. The decoder marks the `BRNE` of each such loop and stores the counter's registers, the step and the cycles of a pass in the op. Each time a run is back at the loop's head, it counts the passes that end before the next event or the limit, and the passes that leave the counter above zero. It adds the cycles of all but the last of the smaller count, takes their steps off the counter, and runs that last pass op by op. The jump is exact for the reasons above, and for one more. A jumped pass leaves `SREG` as it was at the head, but the pass that runs next rewrites every flag the loop writes before any is read. `SUBI`, `SBIW` and `DEC` read no flag, each `SBCI` reads only the `C` and `Z` the op before it wrote, and `BRNE` reads that pass's `Z`. That pass ends before the event, the limit and the exit, so no run returns, takes an interrupt or leaves the loop with the stale flags. A loop that also stores or reads I/O, or that tests its counter with `CPI` or `CP`, is not a countdown and steps as before.

`tests/free_runs.rs` compares both runs with a loop of single `Cpu::step` calls. It checks an interrupt, a timer event and a run limit at every cycle offset of a free run, `SEI` with an interrupt pending, a run that starts just after `SEI` or `RETI`, a host poke of `EERE` awake and before a wake, a host poke of every I/O register with ten values, awake and asleep, a byte stamped just before a free run, and the shipped firmware with the interrupt-heavy timing programs. `tests/steady_runs.rs` does the same for skipped passes. It puts the end of a wait at every offset of a pass: a timer flag, an SRAM flag an ISR sets, an ADC conversion, a USART shift, an SPI byte, an EEPROM write and a TWI phase, and `INTF0`, `ACI` and `firmware/button.S`'s loop after a host drive or voltage. It puts a run limit at every cycle of a poll and of each wait's end, and runs loops that count, flip a flag, or read `TCNT0`, `UDR0`, `ADCL` or `SPDR`, which must not be skipped. It starts a run on a steady load just after `SEI` or `RETI`, and follows a host drive of `PC7`. `tests/counted_runs.rs` does the same for countdown loops. It puts a run limit at every cycle near the start, near each byte borrow, zero byte and sign bit, and near the exit, a timer overflow at every offset of a pass both at the start of a trip and thousands of passes in, and a host write of the counter between runs. Its loops count in r0, r15, r16, r20, r24, X, Y and Z, and one runs `NOP`s out to the farthest `BRNE`. One test sets `T`, `H` and `C` before each loop, so a jump must keep the flags the loop never writes. It runs nested loops and bodies that must not be jumped. A loop of 2^32 - 1 passes ends in one run from a free run and from a steady one, and the test fails in 30 s when the jump does not fire.

## Model parameters

The documents leave four timing points open or spread across figures. The core fixes each one.

1. **A clock-select store takes effect at the instruction's retiring edge.** A store lands on the clock edge that ends its instruction (Figure 14-4), and the timer is a synchronous design clocked through an enable (§15.8). So every timer clock up to and including that edge runs with the old select, and the new one counts from the next edge. Those clocks do everything a timer clock does: they count, raise `TOV` and the delayed compare flag, drive the OC pin, and copy a buffered `OCR`. An `OUT` to `TCCR0B` that ends at edge 8 gives a /1 timer its first count at edge 9. A `GTCCR` store resets the prescaler on the same edge, after the timers have run their clock there, so a `PSRSYNC` store that ends at edge E gives a /N timer its next count at E + N (§17.2, §17.4.1).
2. **A flag raised on an instruction's last cycle is served at that boundary.** §7.7.1 finishes a multi-cycle instruction before the interrupt is served. A flag that rises on the edge that ends an instruction is pending at that boundary, so the next thing the core does is the four-cycle entry. A flag that rises inside a two-cycle instruction waits for it to finish, so an interrupt period is one cycle longer or shorter than the timer's, by where the program's loop is when each flag rises.
3. **The prescaler counts 0 at the first instruction.** §17.2 makes the prescaler free running and puts the first count 1 to N+1 cycles after the timer is enabled, but gives no phase. The core starts the count at cycle 0, and again at the cycle a reset releases. So the first /64 count is cycle 64.
4. **The OC pin changes on the clock where TCNT reaches OCR, and the flag one timer clock later.** §15.5 sets the compare flag "at the next timer clock cycle" after the match, and Table 15-3 drives the pin from the match itself. Timer1 and Timer2 follow the same rule (§16.7, §18.5). In CTC with OCR0A = 99 at /1, OC0A toggles on the 99th count. In fast PWM a non-inverting OC0A is high while TCNT0 runs 0 to 98, which is 99 counts. The two extremes follow the datasheet's own cases. OCR0A = BOTTOM is the narrow spike of §15.7.3, one timer clock high each period. OCR0A = MAX is a constant output (§15.7.3, Table 15-3 note 1): the match is ignored and the pin sets at BOTTOM. So OCR0A = 0 and OCR0A = 1 give the same waveform, one clock high, and OCR0A = 254 is 254 clocks high while 255 never falls. In phase-correct PWM, a non-inverting pin is continuously low at OCR = 0 and continuously high at OCR = TOP, and an inverted pin is the opposite (§15.7.4, §16.9.4, §18.7.4). The double buffer copies at TOP (§16.9.4). Timer0 and Timer2 state the clear when OCR leaves TOP (Figure 15-7, Figure 18-7). §16.9.4 does not, and Timer1 follows them. A store that drops OCR below TOP clears a non-inverting pin on the TOP that copies the buffer, the first TOP after the store.

Other choices that the documents do not settle are written beside the code that makes them. The watchdog count starts at the store that sets `WDE`. SRAM is zero at power-on. The SPI clock's first edge is half a period after the `SPDR` store. The adapter writes Table 30-1's 1 uA leakage bound as a resistance from each port pin to each rail, so the pin leaks to both rails alike.

## Not yet modelled

- **TWI slave mode.** `TWAR` and `TWAMR` are stored and logged, and nothing answers an address, general call included. The TWI is a master only.
- **SPI with a part on a net.** SPI drives SCK, MOSI, and MISO, but its peer is inside the crate. A byte queue answers a master, or a host master clocks a slave.
- **The analog comparator through the seam.** The adapter hands the core each pin as a level from its watch, so `AIN0`, `AIN1`, and an ADC channel the comparator reads through `ACME` compare 0 V or VCC, not the pins' voltages.
- **`tRST`.** Table 29-11 (§29) gives `RESET` a minimum pulse width of 2.5 us. The adapter does not filter it and resets on a low of any length.
- Timer2's asynchronous clock from a TOSC crystal. `ASSR.AS2` is stored and logged, and Timer2 counts `clkI/O`.
- The boot section. The image runs from word 0, so parse rejects a programmed `BOOTRST`, and `SPM` is logged but does not write flash.
- The TWI slew-rate limiter (§22.5.1). A low edge falls through the pin's 25 ohm stage.
- The TWI spike filter (§22.5.1). The part suppresses a spike under 50 ns. The model has no filter, so it passes any pulse the circuit resolves.
- tHD;DAT's maximum below about 18 kHz. `D` is `T/16`, which stays inside Table 29-14 down to that rate.
- SMBus timeouts. The datasheet does not define them for this part.

## The TWI

The TWI master runs the bus bit by bit on `PC5`, SCL, and `PC4`, SDA. While `TWEN` is set it pulls a line low through the pin's low stage and otherwise releases it, whatever `DDRC` holds. Table 14-7 keeps the pull-up on while the TWI pulls the line low, because `PUOE` is `TWEN`. The pull-up is the netlist's `pull_up_resistance`, 35,714 ohm by default, from Figure 31-348, inside the 20 to 50 kohm range. The model drops that pull-up during the low. On a 4.7 kohm bus with the default, the low is about 3.5 mV lower than the part, and the pin draws about 140 uA less. While it releases a line, the pull-up follows `PORTC` and `PUD`. `Wire.begin` sets both pull-ups. It reads both lines from the pads on the cycle they change, with no `PINx` synchronizer.

§22.5.2 gives the period, `T = 16 + 2·TWBR·4^TWPS` cycles, and Table 29-14 the minimums, but neither says where each edge sits inside a bit. The core fixes a bit table. `S` is `floor(0.45·T)`, `L` is `T - S`, and `D` is `floor(T/16)`. At `TWBR` 72, `T` is 160 cycles, 100 kHz at 16 MHz, and `S`, `L` and `D` are 72, 88 and 10. At `TWBR` 12, `T` is 40, 400 kHz, and they are 18, 22 and 2. `w` is the cycle a `TWCR` write retires.

- START on a free bus: SDA low at `w + L`, SCL low `S` later. `TWINT` sets with that SCL fall.
- Each bit, from the SCL fall `f`: SDA changes at `f + D`, SCL is released at `f + L`, SDA is sampled on the cycle SCL reads high, and SCL goes low `S` after that. A byte is nine bits, `9·T`.
- STOP: SDA low at `w + D`, SCL released at `w + L`, SDA released `S` after SCL reads high. `TWSTO` clears on the cycle SDA reads high.
- Repeated START: SDA released at `w + D`, SCL released at `w + L`, SDA low `L` after SCL reads high, and SCL low `S` after that, `T + L` in all.

A length after a release counts from the cycle the line reads high, so a device that holds SCL low stretches the bit (§22.4). A length after the TWI pulls a line low counts from that cycle. Another master's earlier SCL fall pulls this one's SCL low at once, and another master's START while this one waits to send its own is joined. SCL stays low while `TWINT` is set (§22.5.5).

A master read releases SDA for the eight data bits and drives the ninth from `TWEA` (§22.7.2). A 1 the TWI sends that reads back 0 at its SCL rise loses arbitration. The status is 0x38, both lines are released and `TWINT` sets. An SDA change while SCL is high inside a byte is a bus error, 0x00, with both lines released (§22.8). A START seen on the bus makes it busy and a STOP frees it. A START asked for while the bus is busy waits for the STOP, then starts `L` after it. Wire's writes at 0x38 and 0x00 are rows of Tables 22-2 and 22-6 that put nothing on the bus. A `TWCR` write that is a row of no table, including `TWSTA` with no `TWSTO` from 0x08, 0x10, 0x40 or 0x50, is logged once. This master releases both lines and drops the phase start it had recorded. If it owned the bus, it treats the bus as free. If it did not, `busy` stays with the bus monitor, an SDA edge while SCL is high, and a START waits for a STOP when the bus is busy.

`TWAR` resets to 0xFE (§22.9.5). Each phase the TWI finishes is an `I2cStamp`: its first bus change and its end in oscillator cycles from power-on, and what it was. `Cpu::take_i2c_through` takes the phases that ended by a cycle. An in-run reset keeps the phases that ended and drops the one on the bus. `traces/i2c.csv` is written from them, as [run-folder.md](run-folder.md#traces) says.

`tests/twi.rs` runs the TWI against a peer on its pads that holds the bus high, answers as a target, and records every edge. At `TWBR` 72 and 12 it checks every interval of the bit table, repeated START included, against Table 29-14's timing rows. It also covers master transmit and receive, each master status code Wire's `twi.c` reads, clock stretching, arbitration loss, both bus errors, and a START that waits for another master's STOP.

## Behind the seam

`Timeline` runs the core on a host's clock. Its time is the oscillator's cycle count from power-on, which no `CLKPR` write changes, so the host converts it with the one crystal rate. Inside the crate each pin report converts through the `CLKPR` epoch in force at its cycle. `Cpu::run_to_news(limit)` returns after the first step that leaves a pin report, an ADC sample the host must answer, or a new division, and a start-up, a held reset, or a sleep coasts no further than `limit`. `Timeline::run_until(limit)` halts at the first oscillator cycle in `(reached, limit]` where a pin's drive changes or the ADC samples. The core runs whole instructions and may sit a few cycles past a halt, but each drive change keeps its own cycle, so a run split at any limit is the same run.

§9.11 puts a new `CLKPR` division in force between `T1 + T2` and `T1 + 2 * T2` after the write, where `T1` is the old period and `T2` the new one, and two active clock edges fall in that interval. The core runs the `STS` that writes it and one more CPU cycle at the old division, then counts at the new one. The two edges after the store retires are then `T1` and `T1 + T2` after it, so the second is on the window's opening whether the clock slows down or speeds up. In `firmware/clkpr.S`, /1 to /2, the store retires at oscillator cycle 8, the window is 11 to 13, and the `SBI` after it toggles PB5 at 11. In `firmware/speedup.S` under `CKDIV8`, /8 to /1, the store retires at oscillator cycle 64, the window is 73 to 74, and the toggle is at 73. The new division begins a cycle past the one the core stops on after the store, so the core converts between CPU and oscillator cycles across its last two divisions.

The ADC asks the host for its volts at sample-and-hold. The timeline halts there, and the host answers before the conversion completes. An input reaches the pad at the CPU cycle the timeline reached, whatever instruction the core ran across that cycle, a `CLKPR` store included, so the synchronizer, `INT0` and `INT1`, the pin-change groups, input capture, and the `T0` and `T1` inputs all count from that cycle (§14.2.4: only the synchronizer adds latency). What they made by the cycle the core sits on, a flag, `ICR1`, or a count, is in place before the next instruction, and an interrupt it raised is taken at that boundary unless `SEI` or `RETI` just ran (§7.7.1). The host lands every input of one instant before the core takes one, so the lowest vector among them runs first. What an input causes comes at least one CPU cycle after where the timeline reached.

Two inputs still land where the core sits, up to an instruction's length later. A read inside the instruction that ran past the input's cycle, of `PINx` or of a flag, has already read the old value. A low on `RESET` and a brown-out reset the chip at the instruction boundary the core is on.

`crates/circuit/src/active/avr.rs` is the adapter, `active::Core::Avr`. It builds its chip from a netlist's `atmega328p` part, which [netlist.md](netlist.md#atmega328p) defines: the 28 package pins in DIP order, the output stages, both pull-ups, the I/O and `RESET` input thresholds, the crystal in hertz, the fuse bytes, and the flash image. `PACKAGE` gives each package pin's kind in the order of the part's pin names, and every lookup derives from it. Parse admits only the fuse settings the model runs, as [Fuses](#fuses) says, so the clock is a crystal or ceramic resonator (Table 9-1, `CKSEL` 0110 to 1111), and the crystal holds `PB6` and `PB7`. The other 21 port pins, `PC6` among them, are each an output in the image and a watch, in package order.

- A drive maps to the seam's drive one for one. `PullUp` stamps the pin's pull-up to the chip's own VCC pin, the reset pull-up on `PC6` while it is `RESET`.
- Every port pin, `PC6` as `RESET` included, stamps its leakage to the chip's own VCC pin and again to its GND pin in every drive state. So each port pin's DC paths to both rails, which `net.floating` counts, are in every solve.
- A watch reads its pin against fractions of the chip's own supply, `V(pin) - f * V(VCC) - (1 - f) * V(GND_8)`. It rises above the high fraction and falls at or below the low one. A flip drives the pad at the CPU cycle of its picosecond, as above. `PC6` while it is `RESET` reads against `RESET`'s own thresholds, and low holds the chip in reset and high releases it. RXD and TXD are those pads. A `serial` source or another chip's TXD drives RXD, and TXD drives the net it joins. `Cpu::supply` stays the crate's test host.
- With brown-out fused on, one more watch reads `VCC - GND_8` against the fuse's `VBOT+` rising and `VBOT-` falling (Tables 29-11 and 29-12). Below the window the chip holds in reset and every port pin is off.
- Each watch is a logic signal, its level the one the core takes. A port pin's is named for the pin, so `PC6` keeps its name while it is `RESET`, and the brown-out watch's is `VCC`. They are in name order, `PB0` to `PD7`, then `VCC` while brown-out is fused on, the order of their refs. The brown-out watch has a signal because it is the comparator that resets the chip, and `#net.vcc` alone does not show when its window, 25 mV either side of `VBOT`, trips.
- The samples are ADC0 to ADC5, `AVCC`, and `AREF`, each against `GND_22`, read on the step's solution at the stop, before any change there.
- A run to `limit_ps` runs the timeline to oscillator cycle `floor(limit_ps * f / 1e12)`, and a halt at cycle `c` is a stop at `ceil(c * 1e12 / f)` ps.

The values are the part's own fields. [netlist.md](netlist.md#atmega328p) gives their defaults and Table 30-1's wider limits. Each default is a typical eye reading of a DS figure but `leakage_resistance`, which is derived from a bound.

The tests run firmware written for the repo. `firmware/*.S` is the source, and the committed `firmware/*.hex` is what they read. `scripts/firmware.sh` rebuilds the hex with `avr-gcc` and `avr-objcopy`, and a build of the engine never needs them.

Ten seconds of `firmware/blink.S` at 16 MHz are 21 stops and 20 edges on PB5. The first stop, at cycle 2, is `SBI DDRB,5` turning PB5 from off to driven low. It changes the image, and on a pin its load already holds low it does not change the level. `blink_throughput` (ignored, in `crates/circuit/src/tran/tests/avr.rs`) times those 160e6 cycles on the bare core.

`examples/atmega-blink` runs `firmware/blink.S` for 4 s with an LED on PB5, and `crates/cli/tests/blink.rs` checks its edges and the LED's current. The core jumps the passes of its delay loop, so its 64e6 core cycles are a small part of a release `sim run` of it.

`examples/atmega-button` runs `firmware/button.S` for 1 s with that LED and a push button from PD2 to ground. The pin's pull-up holds PD2 high while the button is open, and the loop copies the inverse of PIND2 to PB5. `crates/cli/tests/button.rs` checks those edges and the LED's current. Each pass reads two ports and writes `PORTB`. A store that changes neither DDR nor PORT does no pin work, unless a poke planted the wires since the last reconcile. `button_throughput` times its 32e6 cycles on the bare core and through the seam.

`examples/atmega-analog-serial` runs `firmware/analog_serial.S` for 19 ms. A 10 kohm potentiometer sits across VCC and ground with its wiper on PC0. The firmware reads ADC0 against AVCC and prints the code as decimal on TXD, one line per reading. `U2X0` stays clear and `UBRR0` is 103, so a bit is 16 * (103 + 1) cycles, 104 us at 16 MHz (Table 20-1 and Table 20-6). The run prints three lines. `crates/cli/tests/analog_serial.rs` checks the wiper voltage and those frames. [bench.md](bench.md) times the committed run and the `live` runs of this example. The table there is from engine 0.6.0. Serial output is the TXD waveform in `logic.vcd`, and `traces/serial.csv` holds each byte with its start-bit time, as [run-folder.md](run-folder.md#traces) says.

`examples/atmega-serial-echo` runs `firmware/serial_echo.S` for 12 ms. A `serial` source named `vrx` drives PD0 through 1 kohm, and PB5 drives the WP7113ID of `examples/led-5v` through 220 ohm. The firmware sets `U2X0` clear and `UBRR0` to 103, enables `RXCIE0`, `RXEN0` and `TXEN0`, and waits. The RX vector reads `UDR0`, sets PB5 on the byte `'1'`, clears it on `'0'`, and writes the byte back once `UDRE0` is set. The events send `"1"` at 1 ms and `"0hi"` at 5 ms, both at 9600 baud. A byte sent before 8.125 ms lands after `"0hi"`. Two such bytes echo before 12 ms, and a third does not. `crates/cli/tests/serial_echo.rs` checks the hex, the four scripted echoes in `traces/serial.csv`, the LED, and a byte typed on the first frame. Each echo starts after that frame's `RXC0` and within the vector plus one bit. PB5 rises after `'1'` and falls after `'0'`. The same netlist with the host at 115200 returns 255 and never raises PB5.

`examples/atmega-fade` runs `firmware/fade.S` for 1.57 s with the WP7113ID of `examples/led-5v` on PB1. Timer1 is 8-bit phase-correct PWM, prescaler 64, a period of 32640 cycles, 2.04 ms at 16 MHz (§16.9.4). `OCR1A` steps by 5 every 480,000 cycles, 30 ms, and reverses at 0 and 255. `crates/cli/tests/fade.rs` checks the PB1 edges and the LED's current. The run writes 15701 rows and 1481 PB1 edges. [bench.md](bench.md) times the committed run and the `live` run of this example. The table there is from engine 0.6.0.

`examples/atmega-board-blink` is the development board fragment. It runs `firmware/sketch/blink.hex` for 4 s. Header nets use the pin names `d0` to `d13` and `a0` to `a5`. [board.md](board.md) lists the parts and what the fragment leaves out. `crates/cli/tests/board.rs` checks the fragment. `examples/atmega-board-button` is that fragment running `firmware/sketch/button.hex`, with a switch from `d2` to `v5` and a 10 kohm pull-down. The pin 13 LED follows the press. `examples/atmega-board-knob` is that fragment running `firmware/sketch/knob.hex`, with a knob's two contacts on `d2` and `d3`, and the sketch prints a count for each.

## Sent bytes

`Cpu::take(Channel::Usart0)` returns each byte the USART finished, with the CPU cycle its last bit ended. `Cpu::take_sent(through)` pops the same queue and returns a `Sent`: the USART's index, the byte, the oscillator cycle its start bit drove `TXD` low, and the oscillator cycle its last stop bit ended. It returns only asynchronous frames whose end is at or before oscillator cycle `through`. A Master SPI mode byte has no start bit and is not serial output, so `take_sent` drops each one ahead of a frame and keeps scanning. `Timeline::take_sent` is the same call on the timeline's clock.

The USART stamps both cycles on the oscillator when they happen, through the bus's last two `CLKPR` divisions. Those two convert only a cycle at or after the start of the earlier one, and a slow frame can start before both divisions of two `CLKPR` writes, so a stamp converted later could be wrong. A stamp taken on its own cycle is always inside the window. A gated USART clock, from sleep or `PRUSART0`, moves the shifter's deadlines and leaves the start where it is, because that time has passed.

A host on a fixed oscillator of `f` hertz puts a frame's start at `ceil(start * 1e12 / f)` ps. That is the time of the core's stop for the `TXD` drive change on that cycle. A fall shows in `logic.vcd` at that time only when it is the pin's net level change in that picosecond on a pin nothing else loads. A start bit driven on the cycle the transmitter takes a `TXD` that already reads low has no fall, and a capacitor on `TXD` moves the crossing past the drive.

A watchdog, brown-out or `RESET` reset keeps the bytes the USART had already finished, as on hardware. The frame still in the shifter or the buffer is cut off and dropped. A byte whose stop bit ends on the reset's own cycle is kept. A watchdog reset comes after every event up to its cycle has fired. A brown-out and `RESET` reset at the instruction boundary the core is on, which can be a few cycles past the host's time, the same skew a pin's reset level has. A power-on reset restarts the timeline and keeps nothing. `tests/sent.rs` checks each case: two `CLKPR` writes inside a frame, a sleep before a frame and during one, each reset kind on the cycles around a frame's end with several frames sent and one waiting, a power-on reset after two finished frames, and a Master SPI mode byte queued ahead of a frame.

## Fuses

Parse keeps only the fuse settings the model runs, and [netlist.md](netlist.md#atmega328p) lists the rejected ones and why. The core reads these.

- `CKSEL0` and `SUT` pick the crystal's row of Table 9-4 or 9-6: the delay after a reset, and the start-up from power-down and power-save. `CKSEL3:1` picks among the crystal rows of Table 9-1, which the model runs alike. Power-on start-up is over at cycle 0.
- `CKDIV8` programmed starts `CLKPS` at /8 (§9.12.2), so the CPU runs at an eighth of the crystal until the firmware writes `CLKPR`.
- `RSTDISBL` programmed makes `PC6` a port pin (§14.3.2).
- `WDTON` programmed holds the watchdog in system reset mode (Table 11-1).
- `BODLEVEL` 110, 101, or 100 enables brown-out at Table 29-12's typical `VBOT`, 1.8, 2.7, or 4.3 V, with `VBOT-` and `VBOT+` 25 mV either side, half of Table 29-11's typical `VHYST`. 111 disables it.

## Ratings

§29.1 rates the part at 40 mA DC through any one I/O pin and 200 mA DC through the `VCC` and `GND` pins, as absolute maxima. It limits the voltage on any pin but `RESET` to -0.5 V to VCC + 0.5 V with respect to ground, and on `RESET` to -0.5 V to 13.0 V. The netlist kind has no `ratings` object yet, and the run does not check these. A later change checks them.

## The timing set

`crates/avr/tests/timing.rs` is the pass test. Thirty-nine programs written for it are in `tests/timing/asm`, and `tests/timing/hex` holds their builds for the ATmega328P, with no start files or libraries. The header of `timing.rs` gives the command that builds them, and every committed image is byte-identical to a fresh build of its source. Each program marks time with `out PORTB`, which drives the pin on the edge that ends the `out` (Figure 14-4). The test runs each image, reads the pin edges, and checks each row against the value the documents give. The rows cover instruction cycles, pin writes, interrupt entry and return, Timer0 and Timer1 overflow periods, CTC and fast-PWM waveforms with OCR0A at 0, 1, 99, and 0xFF, the compare-to-overflow lag, and clock-select timing. The timing set does not row-check phase-correct PWM, and `crates/avr/tests/timers.rs` and `crates/cli/tests/fade.rs` do.

Every row carries its citation and is exact. There are 107 rows. The interrupt periods and the compare-to-interrupt lags are worked out from each program's loop phase under parameter 2, in `IsrLoop`. A model change that moves a row fails the suite, and a row's expected value moves only when the document reading behind it does.

`cargo run --release -p sim-avr --example throughput` reports how fast the core runs a timing-set program on the build machine. `scripts/check.sh` does not run it.

## Compiled sketches

Every other test runs firmware written in assembly for the repo. `firmware/sketch` holds programs written the way a user writes them, in C++ against the standard AVR runtime, which supplies `main()`, `millis()` and `delay()` on Timer0, `analogWrite` on the three timers, `digitalWrite` through its pin tables, `tone()` on Timer2, and an interrupt-driven `Serial`. Each sketch is `firmware/sketch/<name>/<name>.ino`, and the committed `firmware/sketch/<name>.hex` is its build for the ATmega328P at 16 MHz with the flags the standard IDE uses: `-Os`, link-time optimization, and the runtime's core compiled into an archive and linked with the sketch.

`scripts/sketches.sh TOOLS_DIR` rebuilds every image. It pins a GCC 7.3.0 toolchain for Linux x86-64 and the runtime's 1.8.6 release by URL and SHA-256, fetches each archive once, and unpacks it under `TOOLS_DIR`. A rebuild is byte-identical to the committed hex. The engine and its tests read only the hex, so neither needs the toolchain. `firmware/sketch/NOTICE` gives the runtime's license and its source.

`crates/avr/tests/sketches.rs` runs each image on the bare core and checks what a user would see on the pins, with every expected time worked out from the runtime's own timer and USART setup.

- `blink` holds PB5 high and low for `delay(1000)` each, 16e6 cycles to within one 4 us `micros()` step and the code around it.
- `blink_without_delay` toggles PB5 at the Timer0 overflow where `millis()` reaches each second. An overflow is 1.024 ms, and `millis()` carries the 24 us remainder as a fraction, so it reaches 1000 at overflow 977, 2000 at 1954, and 3000 at 2930.
- `button` copies PD2 to PB5 within a pass of `loop()`.
- `fade` steps the duty on PB1, Timer1's OC1A, by 5 every 30 ms. A pulse is `2 * OCR1A` timer clocks of 510, centered on BOTTOM. At 255 the runtime calls `digitalWrite`, which disconnects OC1A before it sets `PORTB1`, so the pulse it lands in drops low between the two stores before the pin holds high. Hardware does the same.
- `analog_read_serial` prints `analogRead(A0)` at 9600 baud. The runtime sets `U2X0` and `UBRR0` = 207, so a bit is 1664 cycles, 0.16 % fast. The 64-byte transmit buffer keeps every frame back to back.
- `serial_counter` prints a counter at 115200 baud with `UBRR0` = 16, a bit of 136 cycles, 2.1 % fast.
- `tone` plays `tone(8, 440, 500)` once a second. Timer2 runs CTC at /128 with `OCR2A` = 141, and the compare ISR toggles PB0 440 times, each within 128 cycles of an 18176-cycle grid.
- `ws2812` bit-bangs PB0 for 13 LEDs and then holds the pin low. A 0 is high for 5 cycles and low for 15, and a 1 is high for 10 and low for 10, which sits in the WS2812E-1313 windows. `crates/cli/tests/ws2812.rs` checks the colours. The bare-core tests above do not decode the chain.

The timing windows are hundreds of cycles wide, and the serial tests also check frame spacing to the cycle. They catch a wrong timer, prescaler, `millis()` or baud, which moves an edge by hundreds to thousands of cycles. An interrupt entry one cycle late moves edges a few dozen cycles and stays inside the windows. The unit tests in `crates/avr/src/irq.rs` and the `oc_pin`, `system`, `timeline`, `timers` and `timing` suites in `crates/avr/tests` are cycle-exact and catch that.
