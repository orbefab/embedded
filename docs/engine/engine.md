# Engine

This page describes the engine at its `engine_version`. It is not part of the netlist or the run folder format. [netlist.md](netlist.md) says what a document means, and this page says how the engine computes it.

A run folder does not depend on the target the engine was built for, because no float result that reaches it is left to the platform. The `f64` methods `exp`, `ln` and `cbrt` call the platform's math library, and two platforms can round their last bits differently. The engine calls the `libm` crate for them instead, which computes each one in Rust from operations IEEE 754 rounds the same way everywhere. `sqrt` stays the `f64` method, because IEEE 754 rounds it exactly. `clippy.toml` bans the `f64` and `f32` methods whose result depends on the platform, tests included, so a test's closed form is held to the same rule. Every example's run folder is the same on x86-64 Linux and on `wasm32-wasip1`, `created_at` aside, and `packages/engine/scripts/wasi-check.sh` checks it in `scripts/check.sh full`. No other target is measured. A target whose `f64` arithmetic is not IEEE 754 binary64, such as 32-bit x86 without SSE2, is outside this.

## Transient steps

The run solves the operating point at t = 0, then steps to `stop_ps`. Every switch edge, every source jump, every potentiometer move and every world change before `stop_ps` is a breakpoint, and so is every stop of an active chip's core, as [Active chips](#active-chips) says. So is 1 ns after t = 0, and 1 ns after each edge or jump, when that time is before `stop_ps`. A step lands on each breakpoint and on `stop_ps`. A row time is not a breakpoint. Only the row times are written to the trace, as [Rows](#rows) says. Every accepted step also feeds the envelope. A row closes the interval open since the previous row. A step that is not a row folds into that interval. A restart solve, a step the error control refused, and a probe locating a chip event are not samples. The accepted step the probe lands is.

At each switch edge or source jump the step's solution holds the old contact or level. Then the run solves once more at that time, with the new contacts and levels in force, every capacitor held at its voltage, the way an `initial` voltage is held at t = 0, and every inductor held at its current. This restart solve gives the currents just after the change. It is never a row, and the next step starts from it. Each held voltage moves by the capacitor's current times 1 fs over its capacitance. A capacitor that sets `series_resistance` holds that voltage across its capacitance, and the resistance sits between pin `a` and the capacitance. Each held current moves by the inductance's voltage times 1 fs over its inductance. That hold is the backward Euler companion of a 1 fs step, as a capacitor's is. Newton checks a held capacitor by the node voltages at its ends and not by its current, which is the voltage across the hold times its capacitance over 1 fs. On 100 nF one ulp of 5 V there is 9e-8 A, so a 1 pA check on that current passes only when the solve repeats to the bit. The operating point checks a capacitor held at its `initial` voltage the same way. A jump at `stop_ps` gets no restart. A `steps` event, a potentiometer move or a world change is a jump under that rule. At a world change every field the quantity drives takes its new value in the restart solve, as a `steps` source's level does, and a row at that time holds the old value. A world change with no part that reads it still lands and restarts, so its `#world.<quantity>` column steps at its picosecond.

The run reads each part's edges one at a time, in time order, so it never lists a schedule whole. A dense schedule costs steps, not rows. The row cap counts grid rows only. A run accepts at most 100000000 steps, not counting t = 0. The limit sits far above the steps a run whose rows fit needs on ordinary circuits. At this step policy a 1 MHz pin into a 10 ns load costs about 850000 steps per simulated ms. The limit allows about 117 ms of such a pin. The step policy, not this limit, bounds an MCU-rate run. Reaching the limit before `stop_ps` ends the run with `solver.step_limit` at the last accepted time. The solves toward one accepted step, every try, retry and probe between two accepted steps, may take 2^30 multiply-adds, about a second of solving on one core. A circuit too large or too stiff to step within that ends the run with `solver.step_work` at the last accepted time.

Solver tests that need every accepted step use `simulate_steps`, behind the test-only `step-rows` feature. An `orbe-run` build does not have it. The test-only step-rows mode can also stop mid-run with `trace.too_large`, with `t_ps` the step that did not fit, keeping its rows.

## Rows

A row is a probe, never a landing. It is solved from the last accepted step to the row time, with the method, the chip states and the drive images the next step uses, the way a probe locating a chip event is. The run never accepts it. When a step lands at a row's time anyway, on an edge, a core stop or `stop_ps`, the row is that step, which is the same solve. A probe writes no capacitor history, no error estimate, no step size, no chip state, no core, and no logic level. `stop_ps` is an accepted step in every run, so the last row and `observations.end_ps` are that step.

A probe whose solve stalls, is singular, or gives a quantity that is not finite is solved once more from the same accepted step, with Newton started from the end of the step after the row. If that fails too, the row is the straight line from the state the probe was solved from to the accepted step after it, read at the row's time, and the run reports `trace.row_interpolated`. After a restart that state is the restart's solve, not the row of the step before it, and its current source and switch columns read the levels and contacts after the change. The step after the row converged before any probe ran, so it is accepted either way. A probe never ends the run.

Ratings and logic come from accepted steps only. A rating's first time over and its peak are read on accepted steps. A row's logic level is the last accepted level, which is what `logic.vcd` records.

So the grid never changes the run. Two runs that differ only in `grid_ps` take the same accepted steps, stop each core at the same times, locate every chip event at the same picosecond, and write the same `logic.vcd`, `serial.csv`, `i2c.csv`, `link.csv`, `observations.json`, and `solver.*` and `rating.*` diagnostics. At every row time both have, they write the same values. Only two things depend on the grid. A grid with more rows than the trace holds is refused with `trace.too_large` before the run solves anything. A row whose probe fails twice is reported with `trace.row_interpolated`, and a grid with no row there reports nothing.

A row's probe costs one Newton solve, and two when the first fails. A grid much finer than the steps costs a solve per row, and a grid coarser than the steps costs nothing. `max_step_ps` is the only cap on a step.

The step policy is fixed. Every capacitor and inductor is a store: a state that moves at a slope the rest of the circuit sets. A capacitor's state is the voltage across it, and its slope is its current over its capacitance. An inductor's state is its current, and its slope is the voltage across its inductance over its inductance. A segment starts at t = 0 and at each restart solve that moves some store's slope. The run compares each capacitor's current, and each inductor's voltage, on the restart solve with the one on the step's solution. When every difference is within Newton's tolerance on that quantity, `1e-6` of the larger plus 1e-12 A for a current, and `1e-6` of each end's voltage plus 1e-9 V for a voltage, the edge moved no slope. The states are held across a restart, so the points before it still lie on one smooth curve, and the segment goes on with its past points and the step it wanted. Otherwise a segment starts there, and its first step wants 1 ns. A circuit with no capacitor and no inductor starts no segment after t = 0.

A segment's first step is backward Euler. Every later step is the variable-step BDF2 formula through the step's end, the accepted point it starts from and the one before. With `w` the step over the one before, the slope at the end is `((1 + 2w) / (1 + w) * x - (1 + w) * x0 + w^2 / (1 + w) * x1) / h`. Both formulas are L-stable: a mode much faster than the step decays within it and never rings, so no store needs a rule of its own. A slow mode keeps its amplitude to within BDF2's damping, about `(w h)^4 / 4` of it a step on a mode of angular frequency `w`. That damping depends on the steps, not on where the mode sits, so a ring at 5 V decays as one at 0 V does. BDF2 is zero-stable only while a step is under `1 + sqrt(2)` times the one before. When the next step would be more than twice the step that just landed, as after a step a breakpoint, a core's stop or a watch cut short, the point that step started from is left out, and the next step reads the points before it.

Each step's local truncation error is Milne's: the step's state against a predictor of the same order through the points before it, scaled by the ratio of the two formulas' error constants. From a segment's start the predictor is the start's state plus its slope times the step, and the error is half the step's departure from it. With one point before, the predictor is the quadratic through that point, its slope and the latest point, and the factor is `h / (3h + h1)`, `h1` being the step before. With two points before, the predictor is the quadratic through the three of them, and the factor is `h (h + h1) / (h (h + h1) + (2h + h1)(h + h1 + h2))`. The tolerance on a store is 1e-7 of its range plus Newton's absolute tolerance on it. The range is how far the state has moved since t = 0, the step's end included, from its lowest to its highest. It is the same at any level, so a store's tolerance follows its swing and not its distance from 0. The absolute part is 1e-9 V for each end of a capacitor that is not ground, and 1e-12 A for an inductor, so a store whose range is still 0 is held to what the solve resolves. On a nonlinear circuit a store is also allowed how far its state moved on the step's last Newton iteration. Newton stops within 1e-6 of a node's level, and an error under that is the solve's, not the step's. Without it a store on a regulated rail would read Newton's last move as the step's error at any step, and the run would go on at 1 ps. A linear circuit is solved exactly, so there the tolerance does not depend on the level. On a decay BDF2's error has one sign on every step, so the errors of the few hundred steps of a swing add up. At 1e-7 of the range a 10 ns RC edge stays within 1e-5 of its swing.

A step over its tolerance on any store is rejected and retried from the same accepted point. The next try is `0.9 * ratio^(-1/(p+1))` of it, between 0.25 and 0.9, where `ratio` is the worst error over its tolerance and `p` is 1 for backward Euler and 2 for BDF2. An accepted step grows by the same factor, at most 2, up to `max_step_ps`. When the factor is over 1 it grows by at least 1 ps. A step of 1 ps is accepted whatever its error. An accepted step cut short by a breakpoint or by `stop_ps` keeps the step it wanted. A rejected one does not, and its retry grows from the shorter step. With no capacitor and no inductor there is no error, so the step doubles. A step whose Newton solve fails is halved and retried from the same accepted point, down to 1 ps. A failure at 1 ps ends the run with `solver.convergence`.

## Chip events

A chip reads its inputs through watches. A watch is a sum of node voltages times fixed coefficients, minus an offset, and it reads high only above 0. The NE555's are TRIG minus its lower ladder node, THRES minus CONT, and RESET minus GND minus `reset_threshold`. A watch can have hysteresis, with one sum that turns it high and another that turns it low. A relay is a passive chip with two such watches, one for each sign of its coil current. Each reads the current as the voltage across the coil resistance over that resistance, since that resistance carries the inductor's current. It turns high when the current is above `pull_in_current`, and low when it is at `drop_out_current` or below. The contact is closed while either watch is high.

After each accepted step the run reads every watch on the step's solution. If any reads differently than at the last accepted point, the run locates the first picosecond where one does. Each probe re-solves the same step, from the same point with the same method, shortened to the probe time. The probe follows regula falsi on the earliest watch to flip, rounded to a whole picosecond inside the bracket. It turns to bisection once one end has moved twice in a row, and it stops at a 1 ps bracket. The located point is the later end, the first solved picosecond where a watch reads flipped, and the step lands there. A regula falsi probe whose solve stalls is replaced by the bracket's midpoint. A midpoint that stalls ends the run with `solver.convergence`, and a singular probe ends it with `solver.singular`.

A watch cuts the step, and only a stamp change takes a restart solve. At the located point each chip reacts to its levels. If a chip's new state stamps differently, or a switch edge or source jump falls there, the run takes a restart solve at that point the way it does at a switch edge. The step's solution there holds the chips' old states, and so does a row at that time. The held solve after it is never a row. A flip that changes no drive still gets its edge at its picosecond, and takes no restart solve. The segment, its method and its error history continue across it, as they do across a restart solve that moves no capacitor. In `packages/engine/tests/circuits/ne555-led`, THRES drops back under CONT 1 ps after the latch resets, which is such a flip.

Every watch that flips at one picosecond, on every chip, reacts at once against the levels before it, chips in part order. The run then reads the levels on the solve with the new states and reacts again, up to 8 solves. Chips that still change after the 8th are `chip.unsettled`. The logic levels recorded at the point are the ones the chips reacted to. A watch that flipped on the step keeps its flipped level there, and every other watch takes its level on the solve with the new states. When that solve is back across a flipped watch's threshold, the return is recorded at the next accepted step. A monostable with THRES on DISCH does this, since the discharge pulls THRES back down through the hold. Every located event is at a later whole picosecond, so events cannot pile up at one time. An event located at `stop_ps`, or in the step that ends there, is recorded. No restart runs at `stop_ps`.

The operating point at t = 0 settles each chip the same way. It starts from the chip's power-on state, the NE555's latch reset or a relay's contact open, and reacts to the levels it reads. Only the solve that converges at the nominal gmin and source scale is read, never an intermediate rung of the ladder.

Watches are read at accepted steps. A signal that crosses and crosses back inside one step is missed. The error control keeps steps short where a signal curves, and `max_step_ps` caps them. `grid_ps` does not.

Every chip kind keeps four rules. A watch reads node voltages, never a branch current. A passive chip's state holds only what its stamp reads, so every change of state takes a restart solve. An active chip keeps the rest in its core, and only a change of its drive image or its draw takes one. A chip's DC paths are the union over its states, so a path that some state opens, such as a high-impedance output, still counts for `net.floating`. Passive chips have no propagation delay, so two that flip each other at one time can have no states they agree on. Two NE555s, each OUT on the other's TRIG, reset together, then both falling OUTs set both latches while both THRES are still high. That is `chip.unsettled`. A relay's hysteresis keeps its contact from flipping back at the time it moves, because the restart holds the coil current within 1 fs times the coil voltage over the inductance. A relay whose contact latches its own coil therefore settles. When its `nc` feeds its own coil and the coil is powered at t = 0, it does not. Open, the coil current is above pull-in. Closed, it is below drop-out. The operating point reports `chip.unsettled`. When the coil is powered later, it holds its current across each move and the contact buzzes.

## Active chips

An active chip runs a core on the run's clock. The NE555 and the relay are passive. The ATmega328P, kind `atmega328p`, is active, and [avr.md](avr.md) describes its adapter. So is the ESP32-S3, kind `esp32s3`, whose core runs on its 40 MHz crystal, and [esp32s3.md](esp32s3.md) describes it. Its console sends bytes as a USART does. A fault in its core, such as a watchdog that runs out, ends the run with the fault's error at the stop that reports it. Every other active kind is a device on the `orbe-model` contract, as [model.md](model.md) says, and none has a clock. The SPI replay, kind `spi_replay`, sends its transactions as records, as [Records](#records) says. So does the pulse replay, kind `pulses_replay`, which sends its trains as `Pulses` records. The ST77916 panel, kind `st77916`, is an SPI target with a PWM pin, as [SPI targets](#spi-targets) says. It never stops and takes records. The CST816, kind `cst816`, and the SSD1306 OLED controller, kind `ssd1306`, are I2C targets, as [I2C targets](#i2c-targets) says. A `ws2812` chain is a pulse target, as [Pulse targets](#pulse-targets) says. It watches `DIN` and takes `Pulses` on that net, and drives `DOUT` when it has one. A `dht22` is a pulse target on an open-drain `DATA` line, which it watches and pulls low for each low of the reply it queues. The run builds one active chip per such part, in part order. An active chip's stamp reads only its drive image, one drive per output pin, its fixed leakages, and a device's draw. A draw is a current from the chip's supply pin into its ground pin that fades below its hold voltage, as [model.md](model.md) says. It is stamped with its slope at each Newton iterate, so a circuit with one is nonlinear. It does not scale with source stepping, since it is none at 0 V. A pin drives high through its high stage from the chip's own VCC pin, drives low through its low stage into the chip's own GND pin, pulls up through its pull-up resistance from the chip's own VCC pin, or is off. A microcontroller's pin that pulls up or is off may also pull down through its pull-down resistance into the chip's own GND pin, as [mcu.md](mcu.md#pull-downs) says. A stage is a drop from its rail through a resistance. The drop is a source, so source stepping scales it. A pull-up or a pull-down is a resistance alone, so it does not scale. An output with a leakage resistance stamps it from the pin to the chip's own VCC pin and again to its GND pin, in every drive state, off included. A leakage is a resistance alone, so it does not scale either. The core holds everything else, and the run never compares the core.

Before each step the run asks each core where it next stops. A core stops at the first time its image may change, 1 ps after a call that changed its device's draw, at each time it samples a voltage, and at the end of each record it sends. The step lands there. Every step therefore solves with the images in force at its start, and no accepted step is solved with a drive the core has already changed. The step's solution at a stop holds the old image, as a row there does. A sample is read on that solution, before any change at that time. If the image or the draw changed, the run takes a restart solve at the stop the way it does at a switch edge. A stop that leaves both alone lands without one. A stop at `stop_ps` lands, and no restart runs.

A core runs ahead of the accepted time on a copy. The copy is kept when a step lands on its stop, or on a flip of one of its watches. A flip located before the copy's time drops the copy, and the core runs again from where it was last kept, to the flip. A session also keeps every core at each step that lands within one `max_step_ps` of the time the call asked for, unless the call asked for `stop_ps`. A core that does not stop runs its copy to the end of each step, and that copy is kept as it is. A retry shorter than a rejected try lands before the copy, and then the core runs again from where it was last kept to the step. So when a call that did not ask for `stop_ps` leaves the session running, its last step keeps every core, and an input after any number of such calls runs a core again over at most one `max_step_ps`. Nothing kept is ever undone. A kept copy delivers each byte its USARTs sent whose stop bit ended by its time and each TWI phase by its count, and drops its SPI bytes, which no output reads yet. A kept copy also stages each record it sent that ended by its time. An active chip takes a flip at the flip's picosecond, and a flip on a restart's held solve at the restart's time. What a flip or a sample causes comes at least 1 ps later. So an active chip never reacts at the time it reads, takes no part in the settle at one time, and is never `chip.unsettled`. An active chip's logic signals are its watches' levels, recorded as a passive chip's are. A flip is recorded at its picosecond, and a flip only the restart's held solve shows at the restart's time. So a pin the core drives reads the level its own drive makes at the drive's stop. When the held solve is back across a flip the step found, the core takes both at that picosecond, and the return is recorded at the next accepted step, as for a passive chip. Recorded at one picosecond, the pair would be dropped as a zero-time glitch, and a `RESET` or brown-out pulse the core did take would not show. No restart runs at `stop_ps`, so a drive change or a source jump there is not in the run's logic signals.

Every core keeps one contract. A run up to a limit stops at its first stop after where it last reached, or reaches the limit. A run split at any limit is the same run. A clocked core counts time in whole cycles. A clock of `f` Hz runs to cycle `floor(limit_ps * f / 1e12)` for a limit of `limit_ps`, and a stop at cycle `c` is at `ceil(c * 1e12 / f)` ps. Each clock domain counts its cycles from its last frequency change, and power-on is the first. The run checks that each stop falls after where the core reached, and that no sample, input, record or event changes the image. A core that breaks the contract is an engine bug, and the run panics.

## I2C targets

A `cst816` is an I2C target at address 0x15. `SDA` and `INT` are open drain into the chip's own `GND`. `SCL` is an input. The part does not hold `SCL`, so a target that clock-stretches is not modelled. It drives `SDA` low to acknowledge and to send a 0, and it releases `SDA` otherwise. It answers its own address and does not answer a general call. The model answers while idle, including on the first transfer after `RST` rises. Auto-sleep, and the time a part needs to start after reset, are not modelled.

An `ssd1306` is an I2C target at its `address`, 0x3C or 0x3D. `SDA` is open drain into the chip's own `VSS`, and `SCL` and `RES` are inputs, held by neither. It acknowledges every byte written to it, and its `RES` is the reset the steps below name `RST`. [netlist.md](netlist.md#ssd1306) has its commands.

A START is `SDA` falling while `SCL` is high. A STOP is `SDA` rising while `SCL` is high. The target samples a bit on its own `SCL` rise. It moves `SDA` 300 ns after the `SCL` fall that asked for the new level. That 300 ns hold is this model's choice. UM10204 Rev. 7.0 puts the bus timing in Table 11. `tHD;DAT` for an I2C device is minimum 0. Note [2] on that row says `SCL` must cross 0.3 VDD before `SDA` changes. The 300 ns figure in that table is `tf`. `tSU;DAT` is 250 ns, 100 ns and 50 ns. Fast-mode Plus `tVD;DAT` max is 0.45 us. The hold clears the 0.3 VDD fall note with margin, and it stays inside `tVD;DAT` for every mode in the table. The target needs `SCL` low for more than 300 ns. A 20 MHz TWI with `TWBR` 0 holds `SCL` low for 450 ns. That low is shorter than Fast-mode's minimum `tLOW`, and the engine still handles it.

When a move comes due and `SCL` is already high, the target leaves `SDA` where it is, goes idle, and waits for the next START. It reports `chip.protocol` once, rule `sda_while_scl_high`, at the first time. A release it refused waits for the next `SCL` fall, so it does not keep holding the bus, and it does not drive a later byte of the transfer it left. A part in reset lets `SDA` go after the same 300 ns, whatever `SCL` reads, and that release is not the rule.

The engine runs `SCL` and `SDA` for the target and calls the `I2cTarget` methods [model.md](model.md) lists, in this order.

1. The eighth `SCL` rise of the target's own address calls `start` with the time and whether the master reads or writes. Another device's address calls nothing.
2. The next `SCL` fall drives the address ACK when the address was the target's, and leaves `SDA` released when it was not.
3. The `SCL` fall after that ACK calls `read` with the time when the transfer is a read, and releases `SDA` when the transfer is a write.
4. The eighth `SCL` rise of a written byte calls `write` with the time and the byte. `write` returns whether to ACK.
5. The next `SCL` fall drives that level.
6. During a read, the `SCL` rise of the master's ACK or NACK calls `master_ack` with the time and whether the master asked for another byte. A target that samples its pins for the next byte does it here. The following `SCL` fall calls `read` for that byte when the master ACKed. A NACK ends the read.
7. A STOP calls `stop` with the time. A repeated START does not. The first sample after power-up or after leaving reset does not. `RST` low ends any transfer and calls nothing. The device reads `RST` from its own watch levels.

A `cst816`'s first data byte of a write sets the register pointer. Each later write byte, and each read byte, uses that pointer and then advances it by one, wrapping at 256. [netlist.md](netlist.md#cst816) has the registers.

A touch is a breakpoint on the part. The drive image is unchanged at the touch's time. `INT` falls 1 ps later and stays low for 100 us, then releases. That width is a guess. A second touch during the pulse extends the one release. A touch while `RST` reads low is consumed and pulses nothing. `RST` going low restores the register file on that edge. `INT` releases 1 ps later. `SDA` releases 300 ns later, whatever `SCL` reads, on the same hold as any other `SDA` change.

## Pulse targets

A pulse target declares the level its line rests at and how long a rest lasts before the line is idle. The engine watches the line. It owns the line, the idle timer, the undo for a record that starts before an idle, and the cut when a run ends inside a record. It calls `pulse` with a level and the width that level held, and `idle` with the resting level when a rest reaches its idle time. A line that rests from power-on is not timed, so it never idles before its first change. The engine keeps nothing a device shows. A `ws2812` is a pulse target whose line rests low and idles one picosecond past 280 µs, which is its latch. The device keeps the bits and the colours and declares itself an LED chain. The engine lists a part in `leds` and `traces/leds.csv` only when its device declares an LED chain, not because it takes `Pulses`. At each latch the device marks what it shows as changed, and the engine keeps a copy of the colours at that time as a latch row. [Records](#records) gives the windows and the latch.

A pulse target on an open-drain line, such as a `dht22`, drives its line too. Its one output is the drain: low through its low stage, or off. A call that queues a train, as [model.md](model.md#pulse-targets) says, hands it to the engine at that call. The engine plays it from its start: at each edge it stops the core at that picosecond and sets the drain low or off, and after the last level it leaves the drain off. Each edge is a stop, so the analog solve steps to it, and a release rises on the circuit's pull-up from the line's value there. The watch then times the line as it times any other, so the device's `pulse` calls see its own levels between the watch's crossings. The train in play is part of the core, so a copy rewound to a time inside it plays the rest of it from there. An open-drain line takes no `Pulses` records.

A core whose device reads the world lands at each change of a quantity it reads, as [model.md](model.md#the-world) says, so the run it makes from there reads the new value.

## SPI targets

An `st77916` is an SPI target. The engine takes each `Spi` record whose `CS` and `SCLK` nets are the part's own. While `RST` reads low it drops the record, and the panel sees nothing. A record whose lanes include one whose net is not the part's own data pin for that lane is dropped too, and the first such drop is `chip.link_miswired`, naming its sender. The engine hands the panel every other record whole, and the panel decodes the command word, the registers and the pixels. The panel answers no read, so a record that reads gets `0xFF` from it. Its PWM pin takes each `Duty` record on `BLK` as the backlight. A landing's `Spi` records reach the panel before its `Duty` records. Neither changes what the other does.

## Records

A record is a transaction one active chip sends another. It holds its sender, its start, its end, and a sequence number that counts the sender's records from 0. A `Spi` record holds its phases, its data, the lanes the data ran on, what it reads on MISO, the clock, the nets of the sender's `CS`, `SCLK` and data pins, any of which can be none, and whether `CS` is held at its end. A `Pulses` record holds an idle level, timed levels, and the net of the sender's pin, and a `Duty` record a duty, a resolution, a frequency and the net of the sender's pin. At this version the SPI replay and an ESP32-S3's SPI2 send `Spi` records, the pulse replay and an ESP32-S3's RMT send `Pulses` records, the ST77916 takes `Spi` records and `Duty` records, and a `ws2812` takes `Pulses` records on its `DIN` net. An ESP32-S3's LEDC sends `Duty` records.

A record's end is a stop of its sender's core, so a step lands there. A record changes no image, on its sender or on a receiver, so a stop for a record alone lands without a restart. An ESP32-S3's LEDC update that moves its pin's drive moves it at a stop of its own 1 ps after the `Duty` record's end, so the record and the drive change never land together. A chip takes a `Spi` record when its own `CS` and `SCLK` pins are on the record's nets, so a record with no net for either goes to no chip, a `Duty` record when its own backlight pin, an ST77916's `BLK`, is on the record's net, and a `Pulses` record when its own `DIN` pin is on the record's net.

A landing runs in two passes. The first only reads. Each sender whose core stopped at the landing's time holds the records that end there, and each record goes to every chip that takes it. The second pass is the only one that runs a core again or keeps one. It gives each core its sample, its flips and its records, and keeps it. A target answers a `Spi` record that reads while it takes it, and once every core has taken its records, the engine hands each sender stopped there the bytes for its record, on the kept core and on its copy run ahead. A receiver takes the records of one landing in the order they ended, then by sender name, then in the order its sender sent them. So a receiver listed before its sender still takes the record at its end, and part order changes nothing a receiver takes.

The copy kept at a record's end stages it, with every record its sender sent that ended by then. Each chip keeps how far it has staged its records, and only a kept copy moves that, so a record is staged once, across rewinds, restarts and inputs. A core run again from where it was last kept makes only records after that time.

A display part makes a row of `display.csv` when it closes a pixel write. It closes one only on a record it takes, so a row's time is the landing's, and the copy kept there stages the row. A reset on `RST` drops an open write with no row, so a flip makes no row. Rows are staged once, as records are, and a recorded session keeps them in the order they closed, then by part name, then in the order each part closed them. A display part keeps its frame memory in its state, in pages of 8 rows. Each page keeps the generation of its last write, and keeping a copy copies only the pages whose generations differ, so a commit costs the pages a run touched, not the whole frame.

A `ws2812` takes the next 24 bits for each LED. Without `DOUT` it discards the bits past the last LED. With `DOUT` it passes each high past them on to `DOUT` after its delay, as [netlist.md](netlist.md#ws2812) says, but not a high a `Pulses` record carried, which has ended by the time the record lands. That is `chip.protocol` rule `dout_record`. Green is the first byte, then red, then blue, most significant bit first. A bit is a 0 or a 1 by its high alone, T0H 220 ns to 400 ns or T1H 580 ns to 1 µs, and any low up to 280 µs ends it. T1H is the Worldsemi WS2812E-1313 window. T0H ends at 400 ns, that sheet's 380 ns extended to the SK68xx T0H maximum, which is still below the 1-bit window. A high in neither window is dropped, and the first one is `chip.protocol` rule `bit`. A low longer than 280 µs, RES, latches, and the row's time is the fall plus 280 µs plus 1 ps. Exactly 280 µs is not a reset. [netlist.md](netlist.md#ws2812) gives each number's page. The latch is not a drive change. A chain with a `draw` draws for the new colours from 1 ps after it, at a stop that takes a restart, and a record that replays or undoes a latch draws from 1 ps after the record lands.

A `Pulses` record runs its levels from its start. After the last level the line is back at the record's idle level. A line held at its idle level, from power-on or after a record, starts its next high at the next record's start, so an idle-high line does not stretch the first high. A held line under a record that opens on a low falls at the record's start, and that fall ends no bit. A held line that falls on its pin ends no bit either, so a power-on high that firmware drops to an idle low is no `chip.protocol` warning. That fall arms the reset, as any fall does. A first bit whose high began at power-on, with no rise, is no bit either. A high that a record ends on is a bit, and it keeps its own start when the next record opens with its low. A reset that falls inside a pulse latches there, before the record ends. A low that runs past a record's end arms a stop at its reset, as a fall on the pin does. A record lands only at its end, so the next record may have started before that stop. When it has, the chain goes back to where it was before that latch and runs the record from its start, so a frame split across records latches once, as the whole frame does. Until that record lands, `leds` shows the early latch. When the run ends inside a record, the chain takes the levels the record sent up to the end, with the same going back. A level that starts exactly at the end is taken, as a landing there takes the return to idle, and a record that starts exactly at the end counts as sent. So the ended session and its run folder show the line as it was at the end, whether the frame was one record or two.

The rows live on the core, shared between the kept core and its copy, so keeping a copy copies none of them. A recorded session writes them at the end, ordered by time, then by part name.

## Sessions

A session is a transient run held between calls. It opens on a netlist whose analysis is `transient`, and solves the operating point at t = 0 and the step after it. It keeps no trace. An `op` analysis has no time to advance and opens no session. A circuit too large for the solver opens none, with `solver.too_large`. Neither does an operating point at t = 0 that fails to settle or gives a quantity that is not finite, and the diagnostic is the one a run reports. A grid with more rows than a trace holds still opens a session.

`diagnostics()` returns the warnings the session has found, in `diagnostics.json`'s schema and order. Once the session ends, they are the warnings its `diagnostics.json` lists. While it runs, a `Pulses` record still in flight is not yet counted, as with `leds`. A bit that record has sent outside both windows shows once the record lands or the session ends. The list holds each static warning, such as `net.floating`, with `t_ps` null. It also holds one `rating.current` or `rating.power` for each rated quantity that has gone over, one for each sustained rating [over now](diagnostics.md#over-now) at the last accepted step, each `chip.protocol` and `chip.link_miswired` a kept core has reported, and each `chip.floating_input` an accepted step found. `t_ps` is the first accepted step over that rating, and for a [sustained rating](diagnostics.md#sustained-ratings) the first whose filter reads over it. `peak` and `peak_ps` run through the last accepted step. `over_ps` runs through that step, not to `now`. A step that is over adds its time when the next step is accepted, so a quantity first over on the last accepted step reads 0. The fault that ends a run is `fault`, and it is not in this list. `trace.row_interpolated` is not in the list either. That warning names a grid row whose probe failed twice, and only a recorded trace has those rows. [diagnostics.md](diagnostics.md) names every warning a folder can hold.

`start` fills the floating-net warnings once, when the session opens. Each accepted step checks the inputs a firmware reads, as [diagnostics.md](diagnostics.md#chipfloating_input) says. Ratings are sampled on each accepted step. The operating point at t = 0 is not a sample, and neither is the restart solve at an edge, since each holds the capacitors at a voltage, as [diagnostics.md](diagnostics.md#ratingcurrent) says. An open session and a recorded session both sample them. `over_ratings` takes a probe and returns the `rating.*` warnings through it, with each sustained rating that is [over now](diagnostics.md#over-now). Between accepted steps the probe is a sample, solved with the drive the cores now hold, that is not kept. Before any step after t = 0 is accepted, the sample is that step, which the session has already solved. A call that accepts no new step leaves the list unchanged. After an input, a finding already present stays, and its `t_ps` stays, except a rating over now, which goes once it is no longer over. The peak and the time over can still grow when a later accepted step goes further over.

`advance_to` takes a time and accepts every step that lands at or before it. `stop_ps` caps the time. A running session then stands at that time, which is `now`. A time at or before `now` does nothing, so `now` never moves back. The step after the last accepted one is already solved, and it waits until a call reaches its time. A call whose time falls inside that step leaves it solved and waiting. The call never cuts the step at its time, which would make that time a breakpoint and let the call times move the steps. It never accepts the step early either, which would put `now` at a time no call asked for. So a session advanced to `stop_ps` in any number of calls takes the steps of one call. `orbe-run run` is one such call, on a session that records every accepted step. `next_at` returns the time that waiting step lands, or `None` once the session has ended. A caller that advances to it accepts that one step and can read its edges before any later step, since an edge or a byte joins the logs only on an accepted step.

A probe reads every net voltage and every part's quantities at `now` in one call. A bipolar transistor reports `ib`, `ic` and `p`. An `nmos` or a `pmos` reports `id` and `p`. A chip and a potentiometer have none, and every other part reports `i`, `v` and `p`. It also reads each world quantity the netlist declares, as the trace's `#world.<quantity>` column holds it. On an accepted step it is that step's row, read before any restart there, and it solves nothing. Between steps it is solved from the last accepted step and retried the way a row is, as [Rows](#rows) says. So at a row time the probe is the trace's row, bit for bit. When both its solves fail, the probe is the straight line and says so. `signals` lists each chip signal, chips in part order. `levels` reads them all at `now` in that order, and `level` reads one, both with no solve. A level is the last accepted level, which is what `logic.vcd` holds at `now`. A probe and a level change nothing.

The same call carries the integral of each analog column from t = 0 to the probe's time, in `refs` order. The unit is the column's unit times picoseconds. Each accepted step adds a trapezoid of that column. The left end is the restart row when the step restarted, and the accepted row otherwise. The right end is the landing row. The addition follows the recorder keeping the span, so a refused step is left out. It precedes the restart solve, so a failed restart is included, and the session ends on that landing row. Between steps the probe adds one trapezoid from the same left end to its own values. That piece is not stored. A caller subtracts two readings and divides by the time between them. With no input applied between the calls, the reading at one time has the same bits for every split of the calls that reached it. An input can change a second reading at that same time, as it can change the probe values. An input can change a reading at `now` only in three cases. The re-solve accepts a step that ends before `now`. A store, a capacitor or an inductor, is stiff on the old held step and not on the new one, or on the new one and not the old. Or the probe's first solve, started from the accepted step, fails, so it solves again with the held step's landing as its starting guess. The integrals are plain `f64` values, and a huge finite column can overflow one to infinity. The run folder does not store these integrals.

The session keeps each change of a chip signal on an accepted step. Those are the changes `logic.vcd` holds, in signal order within one time. The levels at t = 0 are not changes. `edges_since` takes a cursor, `EdgeCursor::default()` at first, and returns the changes after the ones it has read and the cursor past them. It solves nothing and changes nothing. A cursor converts to and from a `u64`, the count of changes since t = 0. A cursor past the end reads nothing and stays. `edge_cursor` is the cursor past every change so far. `levels` read at the same time is the t = 0 levels with every earlier change applied. An unrecorded session keeps that log itself. `forget_edges` drains the changes before a cursor, so the log holds only what the caller has not forgotten. A recorded session reads the recorder's change list, the one `logic.vcd` is written from, and keeps no second copy. `forget_edges` on a recorded session only raises the read floor. The changes stay, so `logic.vcd` is unchanged. Cursors keep counting from the first change. `edges_since` returns `None` for a cursor before the forgotten changes. The log stops at the last accepted step, as a level does, and there is no change between that step and `now`. An input can expose a change before a reader's last time, as it can change a probe. A `Change` is `size_of::<Change>()` bytes. That is 24 bytes when `usize` is 8 bytes, and 16 bytes when `usize` is 4 bytes. While a recorded session runs, its edge log and its serial log both hold every entry through the latest step `advance_to` has accepted. At `stop_ps`, and on a fault other than a failed restart, both hold every entry through `now`. A failed restart sets `now` to its landing. The edge log holds no change at that landing. The serial log holds every byte whose stop bit ended by that landing. An unrecorded session keeps those same entries. `forget_edges` and `forget_serial` drain what the caller has forgotten.

`usarts` lists each USART, chips in part order, then each chip's in datasheet order, as the part's name and the USART's, such as `USART0`. Each call ends by delivering every byte whose stop bit ended at or before the last accepted step, from the copies each core runs ahead on, and appends them to the session's serial log. A byte holds its start-bit time, the index of its USART in `usarts`, and its value. The start-bit time is the first picosecond at or after the oscillator cycle the start bit began, which is where the core stops for the `TXD` drive change. The log stops at the accepted step, as a level does, because the span from there to `now` is not settled. An input solves the held step again, and the new steps can locate a watch flip before `now` that the old step did not show. A byte known only from that span could then be one the run never sends. So a byte reaches the log once a step lands at or after the end of its stop bit, at most one step after it, and at `stop_ps` or a fault the log holds every byte that ended by `now`. The log is in the order stop bits ended, then by part name, then by the USART's index in `usarts`, then in the order its chip sent them, because a byte is known only once its stop bit ends. Parse sorts parts by name, so that index order is the part order a parsed netlist has. On one USART that is also the order bytes started. With two USARTs at different bauds, a byte can start before the one ahead of it. `serial_since` takes a cursor, `SerialCursor::default()` at first, and returns the bytes after the ones it has read and the cursor past them. It solves nothing and changes nothing. A cursor converts to and from a `u64`, the count of bytes read, so it crosses a boundary that has no Rust types. A cursor past the end, from another session or a count the caller made, reads nothing and stays. Each byte is in the log once, across rewinds, restarts and inputs, so a session advanced to `stop_ps` in any number of calls holds the bytes of one call. A frame still on the wire at `stop_ps` or at a fault is not in the log. While the session runs, a batch run of the netlist with each applied input scheduled where it landed holds the session's bytes first, then the ones up to its stop. Once the session has ended, they are the same bytes, because that run takes the steps the session took to the same end, `stop_ps` or the same fault. The step limit is the same for every run and is not in the netlist, so a step-limit fault is the same fault there. Each byte is `size_of::<SerialByte>()` bytes, which is 16. An unrecorded session drains the bytes before a cursor in `forget_serial`, so the log holds only what the caller has not forgotten. The recorded session `orbe-run run` writes from keeps every delivered byte in that one list, and `forget_serial` only raises its read floor. Cursors keep counting from the first byte. `serial_since` returns `None` for a cursor before the forgotten bytes, and a cursor past the end still reads nothing and stays. `orbe-run run` writes its session's log as `traces/serial.csv`. That is the log at `stop_ps`, or after a fault the log at the fault's `now`.

A recorded session also keeps each TWI phase that ended at or before the last accepted step, delivered on the same calls as the bytes. One delivery is in the order phases ended, then in part order. A phase that ends on an input can end within one CPU cycle before a delivery already made, and that row is then written after a row that ended later. `orbe-run run` writes them as `traces/i2c.csv`. A phase can end on an input, such as a STOP on the SDA rise the TWI reads, at a time a delivery has already passed. So each core numbers its phases, the count carries in its copies, and a phase is delivered once by its number, across rewinds, restarts and inputs. An unrecorded session drops the phases.

A recorded session also keeps each record that ended at or before the last accepted step, delivered on the same calls as the bytes. They are in the order records ended, then by sender name, then in the order each sender sent them. `orbe-run run` writes them as `traces/link.csv` when a chip in the netlist can send records. An unrecorded session drops the records.

`display` takes a part name and returns its display part as the last accepted step left it, which is its state at `now`, or `None` when the part is no display part. Its `panel` is the state an `observations.json` `displays` entry holds without the image, its `image` is the frame memory `traces/display-<part>.<format>` holds, and its `pixels` are what its glass shows, as [run-folder.md](run-folder.md#what-the-glass-shows) says. A read changes nothing. `orbe-run run` writes the rows of a recorded session as `traces/display.csv`, and each display part's frame memory and glass at the end as `traces/display-<part>.<format>` and `traces/display-<part>.png`. An unrecorded session drops the rows.

`leds` takes a part name and returns its chain as the last accepted step left it, the `observations.json` `leds` entry, or `None` when the part is no chain. A read changes nothing. `orbe-run run` writes the latches of a recorded session as `traces/leds.csv`. The colours stay on the device, so an unrecorded session can still be read. A read copies only the chain it names.

A fault is any error that ends a run after t = 0, the step limit included. A fault other than a failed restart ends the session at its last accepted step, and `now` stays there. The session solves each step as soon as it accepts the one before it. So the call that accepts the last good step returns the fault, even when its time is before the step that failed, and a session can end as it opens. A failed restart sets `now` to its landing. Every later call returns the same fault. A session that reaches `stop_ps` ends with no fault. Each call returns whether the session is running, stopped or faulted, and `fault` reads the fault.

`apply` takes an input: a part, a time and a setting. A setting is a switch's contact, a level in that field's unit, the bytes a `serial` source sends, or an event a device part declares, by name with its fields, such as a `touch` on a `cst816` with `x`, `y` and `down`. A voltage source reads the level as `voltage`, in volts. A current source reads it as `current`, in amperes. A potentiometer reads it as `position`, the fraction of its resistance from `a` to the wiper. A serial send is 8N1 frames at the baud the input states. The input lands at the first time from the one it asks for that is at or after `now`, after the accepted step, at or after that step's reach, and allowed after the part's last event. With no event that is 1 ps. After a level it is 2 ns later. After a switch event it is the event's bounce window and 2 ns later, so an input too close to the last one is moved, not refused. A serial send lands at the latest of that time and the end of the source's last event. The start bit is at the landing. A send asked for while those frames are still on the wire starts when the line returns to idle. The 2 ns gap a level uses does not apply to it. A device event lands at the latest of the time a contact uses and 1 ps, or 2000 ps after the part's last event. An event equal to the last one still lands. The drive image does not change at that time. One `apply` appends one event and solves the held step once, however many bytes the send holds. The event goes after the part's last one. A switch event takes the bounce parse draws for an event at that index, so no earlier edge or draw moves. The held step is solved again from the accepted step with the new edge among the breakpoints, and any step that then lands by `now` is accepted. A level or a serial start bit is a jump, so that solve restarts, as it does at a switch edge. A part keeps one pending breakpoint however many inputs it takes. `apply` returns the time the input landed and the session's status after the re-solve. A failed restart on that re-solve ends the session at the landing. Any other fault on the re-solve ends it at the accepted step. An input whose re-solve faults stays applied. It refuses an input on a session that has ended, on a name no part has, on a resistor, a capacitor, an inductor, a relay, a junction, an `npn`, a `pnp`, an `nmos`, a chip other than a `cst816`, or a `step` or `pulse` source, on a contact for a source or a potentiometer, on a level for a switch, on a position outside (0, 1) or a position whose leg breaks the resistance rules, on a contact or a level that equals the value after the part's last event, a pending event included, and on one that would land at or after `stop_ps`. A serial send on a resistor, a capacitor, an inductor, a relay, a junction, an `npn`, a `pnp`, an `nmos`, a chip, or a `step` or `pulse` source is refused the same way. A serial send on a switch, a potentiometer, or a plain or `steps` source is refused, and so is a contact or a level on a `serial` source. A device event on a resistor, a capacitor, an inductor, a relay, a junction, a chip other than a `cst816`, or a `step` or `pulse` source is refused. A device event on a switch, a potentiometer, or a source is refused. An event the device does not declare, or one with a field missing, unknown, repeated or of the wrong kind, is refused. An integer outside its span, such as a coordinate outside 0 to 4095, is refused. A serial send with no bytes is refused. A plain source does not become a `serial` source. A send whose frames would not end inside a time the engine can store is refused, as one that would land at or after `stop_ps` is. A refused input changes nothing.

`part_level` reads a part's level as `apply` left it: its schedule with every input appended, the document field it holds and that field's unit, or `None` for a name no part has or a part with no level. `netlist::live_field` names the field an input moves on every part of a kind, a potentiometer's `position`, so the scene reader can check that a part file's live param sets that field. A `steps` source has a level too, but a plain one does not, so a source kind names none. The input moves the field in place: the netlist keeps its parts, and the next step solves with the new value.

`set` takes a world change: a quantity the netlist declares, a time and a value in the quantity's unit. It lands at the first time from the one it asks for that `apply` would land a level at: at or after `now`, after the accepted step, at or after that step's reach, and 2 ns after the last change already in force. Later steps of that quantity are dropped. A step exactly at the landing stays when it already holds the value, and is replaced when it does not. The other quantities keep theirs. One `set` appends one change when the value differs from the one in force from the landing on, moves every field the quantity drives, and solves the held step again from the accepted step, as `apply` does. Dropping steps and leaving the value does the same solve and appends nothing. The change is a jump, so that solve restarts. The quantity's pending breakpoint becomes the next step this leaves. Everything that ran past the landing is solved again from the accepted step, so a `set` gives the run a netlist with that change scheduled gives. It refuses a change on a session that has ended, of a quantity the netlist does not declare, to a value outside the quantity's range, to the value already in force from the landing on when no later step differs from it, and one that would land at or after `stop_ps`. A refused change changes nothing.

The reach is the furthest time any attempt toward an accepted step reached. A step tries its longest first. A rejected or stalled try is shortened, and a watch flip is bracketed inside the try that crossed it. So the accepted step can end before an attempt did, and before `now`. A batch run has the input's edge from t = 0, and that edge cuts every attempt past it. An edge before the reach would cut an attempt the session made, and the batch could accept a step other than the one the session kept. At or after the reach the batch makes the session's attempts, and the held step is solved the way the batch solves it. In most runs the reach is the accepted step, and an input lands at `now`, or 1 ps after it when `now` is the accepted step. After a segment start whose first step was rejected it is up to 1 ns later, and after a watch cut up to one `max_step_ps` later.

`open_json` opens a session on netlist.json bytes, finding each blob the netlist names in the blobs the caller passes, and refuses bytes that are not a valid netlist with what parse reports. A batch run of the netlist with each applied input scheduled where it landed takes every step the session accepted and the step it holds, bit for bit, then takes its own steps to its stop. Once the session has ended, that run takes every step the session took, to the same fault.

`simulate` opens a recorded session on its netlist and advances it to `stop_ps`. A recorded session keeps the trace and the logic history `orbe-run run` writes. Every session keeps the floating-net warnings and the rating findings, an open session included. A recorded session refuses a grid with more rows than a trace holds, with `trace.too_large`, and `orbe-run run` writes a folder that reports it.

## Junctions

Each solve is Newton's method. A diode or LED holds one junction voltage between iterations, a zener holds two, and a transistor holds two, `vbe` and `vbc`. Each is limited with SPICE's `pnjlim` against its own `vcrit`, which comes from the diode's `emission_coefficient`, a zener's forward `emission_coefficient` and its derived breakdown junction, or a transistor's `forward_emission` for `vbe` and its `reverse_emission` for `vbc`. A forward step past `vcrit` by more than `2 * n*VT` is cut to a logarithmic step, and a reverse step is floored. Each evaluation clamps the exponent at 700 and the slope at 1e100. A diode starts at its `vcrit`. A zener starts each junction at that junction's `vcrit`. A transistor starts with `vbe` at its `vcrit` and `vbc` at 0. An iteration in which any junction was limited or clamped has not converged. Neither has one in which a linearised current misses its device equation at the solved voltages by more than 1e-6 of its size plus 1e-12 A. A transistor checks both `ic` and `ib`. A zener checks each junction current. A converged iterate passes through the limiter bit for bit. A diode or an LED puts `GMIN`, 1e-12 S, across its junction and puts no node gmin on its internal node. A zener puts `GMIN` across each of its two junctions and puts no node gmin on either internal node. A transistor's `GMIN`, 1e-12 S across each junction, is in its equations, and its internal nodes take no node gmin either. Node gmin stays on every external net. An `nmos` or a `pmos` holds `vgs` and `vds` and passes each through with no limiter. It starts at 0 and 0. Its convergence check is the drain current. Its `GMIN`, 1e-12 S from drain to source, is in that current. It has no internal node. A linear regulator reads its last branch current and its last input voltage. It has no junction, so it takes no `pnjlim` and no limiting. Its convergence check is the output voltage.

## Elimination order

Each Newton iteration solves its linear system by sparse LU, in pure Rust. The system keeps a pattern: the entries its stamps have filled, which only grows. The first system on a pattern sets the order the columns are eliminated in. A column that a row with one entry pins comes first, in index order. That row is a source's constraint, eliminating on it fills nothing, and the unknown comes out as the source's value with no rounding. The rest go in minimum degree order, on the graph of the pattern plus its transpose: each step takes the unknown coupled to the fewest others, ties to the lowest index, and couples its neighbours to each other, as eliminating it would. A net that many parts touch, such as a supply rail, then comes after the nets it couples, and eliminating it fills almost nothing. A branch current whose diagonal is under 1e-3 of the largest entry in its column is late: it waits until the unknowns it is coupled to at first are taken. That is a held capacitor's current, behind 1 fs over its capacitance. Two held in parallel split their current by those tiny resistances, which only the difference of their rows carries, so the voltage they share is eliminated on one of their rows. The current then pivots on its node's row, as a node of a chain would, so holding every capacitor of a long chain fills no more than stepping it.

Each column's pivot is chosen among the entries left in it that are at least 1e-3 of the largest: a row with one entry first, then a late unknown's row, then the diagonal, then the largest, then the lowest row. An entry within the rounding of the sums that made it is never chosen, and a column with no entry left to choose is `solver.singular` at that unknown. The solver keeps the factors of the latest two systems with different late unknowns, which are a step's and a restart's. The next system on the same pattern with the same late unknowns as kept factors is refactored on their pivots, and each column checks that a fresh choice would pick the same pivot. When one would not, the system is factored again with pivoting. So the factors of a system are bitwise those a fresh factorization gives, and a solve does not depend on the systems solved before it. A stamped value, a factor or a solution that is not finite is `solver.nonfinite` at that unknown, on a linear circuit or a nonlinear one alike. A circuit with more than 4096 unknowns is `solver.too_large` before any system is allocated. On a linear circuit a memo keeps recent systems with their solutions, and a system that repeats is answered from it.

## Accuracy

`grid_ps` sets how dense the trace is and nothing else. `max_step_ps` caps the step. Neither sets the tolerance. Each step above the 1 ps floor holds every store's local truncation error under 1e-7 of its range plus Newton's absolute tolerance on it. The step after every edge starts from the edge's restart solve, and an edge that moves a store's slope starts a segment there. That bound is per step and per store. The error the steps accumulate is measured separately. A 10 ns discharge after an edge stays within 7.7e-6 of its swing at every step. A 1 MHz pin into a 10 ns load takes about 430 steps an edge and stays within 1.1e-5 of its swing. The swing, not the level, sets the step, so a rise and a fall cost the same.

A step at the floor is accepted over its tolerance, so a time constant under about 70 ps is not held to it. A discharge after an edge measures this, as its worst error over its swing at every step:

| Time constant | Worst error |
| --- | --- |
| 1 ps | 0.13 |
| 10 ps | 5.1e-3 |
| 30 ps | 6.8e-4 |
| 50 ps | 2.5e-4 |
| 100 ps | 6.1e-5 |
| 300 ps | 6.6e-6 |
| 1 ns and up | 7.8e-6 |

BDF2 damps a ring by about `(w h)^4 / 4` a step, so a high-Q ring held to its range takes short steps. A series RLC with a Q of 31.6 takes about 11400 steps over 5 ms and stays within 5.6e-4 of its swing, and one with a Q of 316 about 6400 steps within 8.2e-4. An LC tank rung by 1 mV takes the same steps and decays by the same share at 0 V and at 5 V, and that share is what `(w h)^4 / 4` over its steps predicts, within 0.2%.

`packages/engine/tests/circuits/rl-step` steps 5 V across 0.505 H and 70 ohm, a 7.2 ms time constant, and back to 0 V. [`transient.rs`](../../packages/engine/tests/circuit/transient.rs) holds every row within 1e-4 of the swing from the closed form.

`packages/engine/tests/circuits/rc-picofarad` is 1 pF behind 1 ohm on a 5 V rail of 0.2 ohm, which a 0.5 mA load steps at 1 ms. Its mode is 1.2 ps. [`step.rs`](../../packages/engine/tests/circuit/step.rs) holds it to three times the 63 timepoints ngspice-42 accepts on its reference deck run as `tran 5000000000p 5000000000p 0 5000000000p`, at reltol 1e-3, 1e-5 and 1e-9 alike. Its rows stay inside the bound the ngspice comparison holds them to.

`packages/engine/tests/circuits/inductor-open-end` is an 0805 coil, 10 uH and 0.5 ohm, from a 5 V rail with no capacitor into a net that draws nothing, the way an Uno's L2 feeds AVCC. A switch loads the rail with an LED from 1 ms to 3 ms. [`inductor.rs`](../../packages/engine/tests/circuit/inductor.rs) holds it to 1.5 times the 76 timepoints ngspice-42 accepts on its reference deck run as `tran 5000000000p 5000000000p 0 5000000000p`, so that neither the print step nor a step cap bounds its step. Its rows stay inside the bound the ngspice comparison holds them to.
